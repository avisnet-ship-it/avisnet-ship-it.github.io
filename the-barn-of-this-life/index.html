Chapter XXVII — The Righteous Word

(Part III — Integration)

The righteous word is born of presence.

It is not the result of improvisation, nor of strategic calculation. It arises when consciousness is integrated enough to recognize when speaking builds — and when speaking merely discharges. The righteous word is not the one that wins an argument, but the one that preserves inner form and respects the reality of the other.

In integrated life, the word ceases to be reaction.

It becomes an answer.

Responding requires listening.

It requires inner time.

It requires discernment about what is truly at stake.

Many words err not because of their content, but because of their timing.

Spoken too soon, they wound.

Spoken too late, they lose strength.

The righteous word finds the point at which it can be received.

Not every truth needs to be spoken.

But every word spoken must carry truth.

The righteous word is not brutal, nor evasive. It does not use sincerity as a weapon, nor kindness as a mask. It is:

Clear without humiliating.

Firm without hardening.

Careful without being ambiguous.

This balance is not learned by technique; it is born of integration.

The healthy barn sustains the righteous word.

Those who speak from emptiness tend toward excess:

They explain too much.

They justify too much.

They defend too much.

Those who speak from a stocked place:

They can be economical.

They say less, but say better.

The righteous word trusts more in the silence that precedes it than in the strength of the voice itself.

In integration, the word aligns with gesture.

There is no glaring contradiction between what is said and what is lived. This gives weight to speech. The righteous word does not need to convince—it coincides. The other perceives coherence, even without agreement.

The righteous word also knows how to say no.

Not only the explicit no, but the implicit no found in a well-placed limit, in a calm refusal, in the absence of empty promise. Saying no with clarity is a profound form of care, because it avoids false expectations and future resentments.

There are moments when the righteous word is simple:

“I can’t.”

“I don’t know.”

“Not now.”

These words, when spoken with presence, organize more than long explanations.

In integrated life, the word does not seek control.

It does not attempt to lead the other by force, nor to shape perceptions in order to preserve its own image. The righteous word respects the freedom of the other to understand, disagree, or not to welcome. (...)

The righteous word is not constant.

It is necessary.

Speaking all the time dilutes meaning.

Remaining silent always suffocates truth.

Integration allows movement between word and silence with greater precision. This reduces unnecessary conflicts and strengthens possible bonds.
